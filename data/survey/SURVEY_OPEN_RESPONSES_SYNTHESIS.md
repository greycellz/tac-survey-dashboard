## Question 1 — What has felt most overwhelming?

**Coverage:** 115/115 responded, all substantive. Mean response length: 177 characters (longest of any open-response question — respondents had a lot to say here).

### Synthesis

Insurance is the dominant adversary in survivor recovery, by a wide margin. Roughly half of all responses to this question (estimated 50–60 of 115) named insurance specifically — most often as the *worse-than-the-fire* experience. Specific insurer names appear repeatedly: State Farm, Mercury, and Allstate (with Allstate occasionally praised, unlike the others). The vocabulary is strikingly consistent: "bad faith," "delays, denials, and lies," "calculated psychological warfare," "David vs. Goliath." Multiple respondents describe physical somatic responses to insurance correspondence — nausea, nightmares, sweating.

After insurance, the second-most-prevalent overwhelm cluster is the **sheer administrative volume of recovery itself**: paperwork, forms, decisions, coordination across agencies. The phrase "second full-time job" recurs across multiple respondents, as does "spinning plates." A subset describes this as decision fatigue specifically rather than effort burden.

A third major cluster is **contamination ambiguity for standing-home survivors** — primarily Eaton smoke-damage cases. These respondents describe being structurally overlooked: insurance treating them as "0% damaged" when contamination tests indicate otherwise, no regulatory standards for indoor contamination, the "wild west" of remediation. Several link this directly to AB 1642 advocacy and the EFRU contamination dataset.

**Distinctive populations with under-recognized burden:**
- **Renters** (multiple respondents): excluded from rebuilding-focused aid; rental assistance gaps; out-of-pocket replacement costs
- **Seniors and disabled** (several mentions): dependence on others; difficulty navigating digital systems
- **Parents** (~15 mentions): managing children's emotional response, school disruption, especially for kids with ADHD, autism, or developmental needs
- **Caregivers with stacked crises**: spouse illness, parent dementia, layoffs co-occurring with the fire

A small but striking subset describe **stacked crises** where the fire compounds with non-fire-related disasters: cancer diagnoses, JPL layoffs, aging parent decline, spousal cognitive decompensation triggered by evacuation.

## Question 2 — Who or what has been most helpful?

**Coverage:** 112/115 substantive responses. Mean response length: 90 characters.

### Synthesis

Two sources dominate: **family/friends/neighbors** (mentioned by nearly every respondent in some form) and **survivor-led community organizations** (mentioned by 30+). The most-named organizations: Eaton Fire Survivors Network (EFSN), Eaton Fire Residents United (EFRU), United Policyholders, Disaster Recovery Center, Altadena Collective, The Hub, Co-Pilot Project, Team Palisades.

A distinctive sub-pattern within the community theme: **other fire survivors specifically** — particularly survivors of *prior* fires (Marshall Fire Colorado, Camp Fire) — are described as uniquely helpful because they understand both the emotional and logistical realities. Multiple respondents describe a "one step ahead" relationship with another survivor as the most valuable support of all.

**Digital community infrastructure** is the third major category: Facebook groups (especially Altadena-area), Discord, WhatsApp street-level groups, Nextdoor, neighborhood email lists. These serve as both information channels and emotional-companion channels.

**Therapy and faith** appear in roughly equal measure (~15 mentions each), almost always paired with family and community rather than as standalone supports.

A small but important counter-pattern: a handful of respondents — primarily renters and isolated individuals — report that **nothing has been very helpful**. This isn't despair so much as structural: existing aid systems didn't fit their situation.

A nuanced finding: several respondents observe that well-meaning friends and family **can be inadvertently hurtful** through comments that betray they don't understand the recovery experience — "put it on your to-do list," "just go buy stuff," well-intentioned advice that lands as dismissive. The implication: insider-status (another survivor, or someone who has done this work) matters more than goodwill alone.

## Question 3 — What would have made your recovery process easier?

**Coverage:** 114/115 substantive responses. Mean response length: 133 characters.

### Synthesis

The dominant answer is **insurance reform of some kind** — faster payouts, no inventory requirements, full policy limits without negotiation, Department of Insurance accountability, public-adjuster cost reduction. This appears in some form in roughly half of responses (estimated 50+ of 115). Several respondents specifically call out the California Department of Insurance as either corrupt or incompetent.

The second-most-common ask is **a centralized navigation resource**, variously described as a "resilience hub," "one-stop shop," "playbook," "checklist," "bullet-pointed list in chronological order," or simply "a guidebook." This is one of the strongest cross-cutting findings: respondents repeatedly note that information was scattered across dozens of agencies, organizations, and websites with no master sequencing.

Third-most-prevalent: **contamination standards and toxin guidance** — particularly from standing-home survivors. The asks here are technical and specific: testing protocols, remediation steps, threshold standards for CAM-17 metals, government testing of soil. A notable contrarian view in this cluster (from a remediator who has gone through the process) argues that lack of standards has created fear that exceeds the actual risk, and that "houses can be remediated" but webinars and articles inflame survivors against returning home.

Fourth cluster: **specific government action** — FEMA trailers, federal aid release timing, debris-removal coordination, tax-law extensions for disaster settlements, permitting reform.

Smaller but distinct asks:
- **Money, plainly stated** — multiple short responses just say "Money" or "Mor money"
- **More mental health support** — free therapy, grief groups, longer subsidized therapy sessions
- **Population-specific support** — seniors, disabled, renters, families with autistic children
- **Protected time off work** — for the recovery work itself

## Question 4 — How do you currently cope with stress?

**Coverage:** 113/115 substantive responses. Mean response length: 66 characters (shortest of any question).

### Synthesis

Coping strategies cluster into seven recognizable patterns, in rough order of prevalence:

1. **Talking** — to friends, family, partners, fellow survivors, therapists, pastors. By far the most common response (~50+ mentions in some form).
2. **Exercise** — walking, running, yoga, hiking, swimming, dog walks. ~30+ mentions.
3. **Therapy / counseling** — explicitly named ~25 times.
4. **Meditation / mindfulness / breathing** — ~15 mentions, often alongside therapy.
5. **Faith / prayer / spirituality** — ~15 mentions, often as foundation rather than technique.
6. **Escapism / distraction** — reading, TV, gaming, video games, music, "doom-scrolling." ~15 mentions.
7. **Medication** — antidepressants (Lexapro, Lorazepam), sleep aids, anxiety medications. ~10 explicit mentions.

Two distinct counter-patterns are worth surfacing:

**Honest non-coping.** A small but striking subset of respondents describe themselves as not coping well, just pushing it down, barely surviving. These answers are short and unvarnished — "I'm not coping with this at all," "Feel like I'm just barely coping," "I don't really cope; I just push it down and move on." This is research-relevant: among 115 respondents at the one-year mark, a meaningful minority are not in any kind of stable coping pattern.

**Substance use.** A small number explicitly name alcohol — one respondent describes cutting back from daily to twice-weekly drinking; another describes drinking "much more than I should" alongside paralyzing self-doubt. One teenage respondent answers simply "Alcohol, sex." This pattern is small in absolute count but should not be ignored as a research finding.

## Question 5 — If an AI coach could help with one thing, what would it be?

**Coverage:** 112/115 substantive responses. Mean response length: 83 characters.

### Synthesis

AI attitudes in this corpus are **bimodal**: a majority offer practical use cases, a substantial minority explicitly refuses or rejects AI for recovery. Both signals are real; the design implication is that AI tooling cannot assume universal welcome even from a survey cohort that consented to an AI-recovery study.

**The practical use cases cluster tightly into four categories:**

1. **Insurance work** — drafting letters to adjusters, citing California insurance code, understanding policy language, fighting denials. The single most-named AI use case.
2. **Personal property inventory** — itemization, valuation, reconstructing lost-belongings lists from photos or Amazon purchase history.
3. **Resource finding and centralization** — what grants exist, what aid is available, what forms apply.
4. **Specialized navigation tasks** — rebuild planning, tax implications, FEMA processes, sequencing decisions.

**The refusal cluster is sharper than expected.** Roughly 15 respondents either declined to name a use case, expressed active hostility to AI, or detailed substantive concerns. The objections fall into recognizable subcategories: algorithmic bias and privacy concerns (vocal); hallucination/accuracy worries ("AI hallucinates"); a philosophical position that AI cannot substitute for human connection during emotional recovery; and a generational/comfort gap ("I'm a bit of a Luddite"). One respondent — a domain expert who uses AI professionally — gives the most articulate critique: AI for emotional recovery would feel like clicking, not connection, and would not survive contact with real human pain.

**A small but distinctive group requests AI for specifically emotional regulation** — nervous system regulation, in-the-moment grounding, journaling prompts. Notably, this group frames their request with caveats about training data, vetted sources, and accuracy — they want AI but only AI built with rigor.

A small subset of teenagers and older respondents say they would prefer not to use AI at all, with reasons ranging from generative-AI skepticism (teenage) to unfamiliarity (older).

## Question 6 — Anything else you'd like to share?

**Coverage:** 89/115 substantive responses (lowest coverage; many respondents had nothing to add). Mean response length: 157 characters.

### Synthesis

This question functions as both a relief valve (where respondents add what didn't fit elsewhere) and an open-ended ideas channel for product design. Themes here are more diffuse than in other questions.

**Distinctive content clusters:**

- **Detailed institutional advice or critique** — what FEMA did right or wrong, model recovery centers, specific organizations to amplify or avoid.
- **AI-specific commentary expanded** — those skeptical in Q5 elaborate; one detailed critique of AI for emotional support specifically frames it as inherently isolating.
- **Survivor-to-future-survivor advice** — overlapping with Q7 but more personal/reflective in tone.
- **Specific policy and product asks** — free long-term mental health, federal aid release, contamination standards, organized self-care experiences and retreats.
- **Critiques of "support" that misses the mark** — yoga classes using fire metaphors, support groups that don't understand disaster trauma, churches with "trauma-informed" labels that lack training.

A theme worth featuring: **the social fragmentation among survivors themselves**. Several respondents note that block-by-block variation, total-loss vs. smoke-damage divides, Altadena natives vs. new transplants, families vs. individuals — all create barriers to peer connection even within affected communities.

Another distinctive thread: **helping others as a form of self-healing**. Multiple respondents describe the experience of channeling their recovery into helping later-stage survivors as one of the most positive aspects of their own process.

## Question 7 — What advice would you give to someone who just experienced a disaster?

**Coverage:** 112/115 substantive responses. Mean response length: 148 characters.

### Synthesis

Advice-giving is the most consensus-rich of the seven questions. Survivors converge on a small set of imperatives:

1. **Don't go alone — find community and other survivors.** The single most-prevalent advice (~40+ mentions). Frames range from spiritual ("find your tribe") to tactical ("get on the email lists").
2. **It's a marathon — pace yourself, expect it to take longer than you think.** ~30 mentions. Recurring vocabulary: "marathon not a sprint," "give yourself grace," "go slow," "take a breath."
3. **Document everything, get organized, keep records.** ~20 mentions. Specific tactics: dedicated email address for fire matters, photos from family members, spreadsheets, written correspondence only with insurers.
4. **Accept help when offered.** ~15 mentions. Often paired with reflection that pride or independence made this hard.
5. **Be wary of insurance; consider a public adjuster early.** ~15 mentions.
6. **Connect with United Policyholders or specific organizations.** ~10 specific mentions of UP.

**Counter-currents:**

- A few respondents offer **darker realism**. "Don't expect any help." "You will be forgotten 6 months later." "Recovery is a full-time job that you never wanted." These responses come from survivors who feel the system has failed them in specific ways.
- One respondent acknowledges they cannot give advice because they are still in the middle of the disaster.
- Several younger and older respondents offer **simpler, more emotional advice** — "Don't lose hope," "Just stay strong," "It gets better." These come disproportionately from teenage and elderly respondents.

**A small set of remarkably specific, tactical advice posts** stand out as worth surfacing for their concreteness — particularly one respondent who details an entire operational checklist (dedicated email address, scour family members' photo libraries, pay off mortgage with insurance funds, hire architect via AIA, "try not to lose your job").

## Cross-question synthesis (all seven questions together)

Across 115 respondents at the ~12-month anniversary of the January 2025 Eaton and Palisades Fires, the open-response data tells a coherent story about how survivors experience recovery and what would change it.

**Recovery is dominated by adversarial systems, not by trauma symptoms.** When asked what felt most overwhelming, respondents named insurance, paperwork, agency coordination, contamination standards, and decision burden — not fear, grief, or PTSD symptoms (though all three appear). The recovery experience at one year is *administrative-as-traumatic*: the systems built to support survivors function instead as additional adversaries, and the cumulative cognitive load of fighting them is the dominant overwhelm. This is consistent across demographic cuts.

**Community is the primary support modality, and survivor-to-survivor specifically.** Across questions, respondents repeatedly return to fellow survivors — particularly those one step ahead — as the most valuable source of help. Survivor-led community organizations (EFSN, EFRU, United Policyholders) appear in both the "most helpful" answers and the "what would have made it easier" answers, indicating that respondents recognize these as load-bearing infrastructure. Family and friends are also pervasive but are sometimes described as inadvertently hurtful when they lack lived experience.

**Information fragmentation is the most-named *fixable* problem.** Almost every question surfaces some version of "I needed a guidebook." Respondents want a centralized resource, a playbook, a sequenced checklist — and they want it from Day 1. The "resilience hub" framing appears organically across multiple respondents. This is the cleanest product opportunity in the entire dataset.

**AI attitudes are bimodal, and the bimodality is itself the finding.** A practical-use majority would welcome AI for insurance correspondence, inventory, resource finding, and administrative load reduction. A substantial minority — including several articulate critics — explicitly rejects AI, especially for emotional support, with concerns ranging from algorithmic bias to a philosophical position that AI cannot substitute for human connection during recovery. Product design that assumes universal welcome will miss roughly 15% of even this consenting cohort.

**Coping at one year is talking, exercising, and (for a substantial minority) not coping well.** The most-prevalent coping strategies are interpersonal (talking) and physical (exercise). Therapy and faith are widely used. Meanwhile, a meaningful subset of respondents — across demographics — describe themselves as barely coping, not coping, or coping through suppression, alcohol, or dissociation. This is research-relevant at the population level even if absolute counts are small.

**The advice respondents give converges on three things:** find community, pace yourself for a marathon, and document everything. Underneath this is an implicit message about the structural shape of recovery: it is long, lonely, and bureaucratic, and the strategies that work are the strategies that acknowledge those three properties.

**Population-specific gaps are visible.** Renters, seniors, disabled individuals, parents (especially of children with developmental needs), and standing-home/smoke-damage survivors all describe being structurally overlooked by systems and discourses oriented around total-loss homeowners. Any aggregate finding from this corpus should be tested against these subgroups separately, because their experience is qualitatively different.

A line from Submission #28 captures the corpus better than any synthesis paragraph: *"Do everything and access every resource quickly. The system does not give you time to grieve."*

## Notes on this synthesis

- All 115 responses were read in full before themes were identified. Prevalence estimates are approximate (e.g., "~50 of 115 mentioned X") and intended to convey rough proportion, not precise count. Researchers seeking exact counts should filter the source CSV directly.
- Only quotes from respondents who answered "Yes" to *May we share these words (anonymously)?* are reproduced. Of the 14 respondents who did not consent, their substantive responses informed theme identification but are not quoted directly.
- Where a quote contains apparent typos or unusual capitalization, these have been preserved verbatim from the source (e.g., "the goverment stepping in," "hourse," "NOT BEING EMBATTLED WITH INSURANCE"). This is intentional — the synthesis is a research artifact, not a copy edit.
- This synthesis describes the corpus at one snapshot (May 2026 analysis of January–April 2026 survey responses). It does not change when dashboard filters are applied; for demographic-specific exploration, use the filtered response list below the synthesis on the dashboard.
